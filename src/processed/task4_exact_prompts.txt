Risk matrix assessment
Policy 1: When considering natural hazard risk associated with subdivision, use or development, the risk level must be assessed using the risk matrix.

[NPS_NH_GUIDE_2026 | clause Part 4: Risk level]
Combining likelihood and consequence
Policy 1 directs that the risk level must be assessed using the risk matrix. The matrix combines the likelihood and consequence of a particular natural hazard event to determine whether the level of natural hazard risk is low, medium, high or very high.
The matrix is a deterministic lookup. It should not be re-derived, approximated or reasoned about qualitatively when the likelihood and consequence levels are known.

[NPS_NH_2025 | clause 1.4]
Interpretation
In this National Policy Statement:
Act means the Resource Management Act 1991.
commencement date means the date on which this National Policy Statement comes into force, as identified in clause 1.2.
decision-maker means any person exercising functions or powers under the Act.
high natural hazard risk means natural hazard risk assessed as 'high' using the risk matrix.
medium natural hazard risk means natural hazard risk assessed as 'medium' using the risk matrix.
risk matrix means the risk matrix, including the likelihood and consequence tables, in Appendix 1.
significant natural hazard risk means natural hazard risk assessed as 'medium', 'high' or 'very high' using the risk matrix.
very high natural hazard risk means natural hazard risk assessed as 'very high' using the risk matrix.
Terms defined in the Act and used in this National Policy Statement have the meanings given in the Act, except as otherwise specified. Terms defined in the National Planning Standards issued under section 58E of the Act have the meanings given in those Standards, unless otherwise specified.

[NPS_NH_2025 | clause 3.2]
Risk-based assessment
(1) Natural hazard risk associated with subdivision, use or development must be assessed using: (a) the likelihood of a natural hazard event occurring, in accordance with Table 1 of Appendix 1; and (b) the consequence of a natural hazard event for life and property, in accordance with Table 2 of Appendix 1; to determine whether the level of natural hazard risk is low, medium, high or very high, in accordance with the risk matrix.
(2) When assessing natural hazard risk, decision-makers must consider: (a) existing and proposed mitigation measures; and (b) residual risk that arises when the design thresholds of any existing and proposed mitigation measures are exceeded.

[NPS_NH_GUIDE_2026 | clause Part 4: Detailed assessment]
When a detailed risk assessment is required
Under clause 3.3(1), decision-makers must have regard to the anticipated level of risk when considering the scale and detail of information necessary.
Most natural hazard data held by a local authority is at region-wide or district-wide scale and is either susceptibility or hazard information. That type of information indicates where an area is likely to be affected by a natural hazard, and may mean that deciding an application requires a request to the applicant for a further detailed assessment of the risk by a suitably qualified and experienced person (SQEP).
Any risk assessment prepared by an SQEP to support a consent application should align with the NPS-NH framework. Depending on the level of risk, it may also be appropriate for the consent authority to have the applicant's risk assessment peer reviewed by an SQEP before deciding the application.
As a general guide, an SQEP is a person who is independent; has a relevant professional registration, such as chartered professional engineer; applies good professional practice; undertakes a risk assessment in accordance with accepted industry practice and guidelines; and is willing to certify and stand by the conclusions of the report.
Regional or district-scale susceptibility mapping is therefore an indicator of the need for further assessment, not a substitute for a site-specific assessment.

[NPS_NH_2025 | clause 2.2 Policy 5]
Best available information
Policy 5: Natural hazard risk assessment and decisions must be based on the best available information and must be made even when that information is uncertain or incomplete.

[NPS_NH_2025 | clause 2.2 Policy 2]
Proportionate management
Policy 2: Natural hazard risk associated with subdivision, use and development must be managed using an approach that is proportionate to the level of natural hazard risk.

[SELWYN_NH | clause NH-SCHED1]
Schedule — Flood Assessment Certificates

1. whether or not the site or activity is located on land that is within a High Hazard Area; and

Risk created or increased on other sites

Provide for subdivision on flat land where the liquefaction hazard has been appropriately identified and assessed, and can be adequately remedied or mitigated.

[CRPS_CH11 | clause 11.3.3]
Policy — Earthquake hazards
New subdivision, use and development of land on or close to an active earthquake fault trace, or in areas susceptible to liquefaction and lateral spreading, shall be managed in order to avoid or mitigate the adverse effects of fault rupture, liquefaction and lateral spreading.
Risk assessment should occur before intensification or other development in susceptible areas. Susceptibility can vary within a mapped area, so site-specific investigation may be needed to establish the actual ground conditions on a particular site.
This policy implements Objective 11.2.1.

[NPS_NH_GUIDE_2026 | clause Part 4: Other sites]
Managing risk on other sites
Clause 3.3(2)(c) requires that subdivision, use or development, including any associated mitigation measures, avoids or mitigates the creation of, or increase in, significant risk on other sites.
Whether that risk is to be avoided or mitigated depends on what response is proportionate to the level of risk posed.
Mitigation works that redirect or displace floodwater are a common way in which a proposal can create or increase risk on a neighbouring site.

[SELWYN_NH | clause NH-P3]
Policy — Areas vulnerable to natural hazards outside high hazard areas
Restrict new subdivision, use, or development of land in areas outside high hazard areas but known to be vulnerable to a natural hazard, unless any potential risk of loss of life or damage to property is appropriately mitigated.
Teaching note. This is the policy that applies to a site that is exposed to a hazard but is not in a high hazard area. It turns on whether the risk of loss of life or damage to property is appropriately mitigated, so a proposal must show what the mitigation is and what risk remains after it.

[NPS_NH_GUIDE_2026 | clause Part 4: Climate change]
Impacts of climate change
Policy 6 requires a risk assessment to consider the potential impacts of climate change to at least 100 years into the future. The 100-year timeframe applies when using existing hazard data and information in the risk assessment process.
If information that includes climate change over the 100-year timeframe is not available or applicable, Policy 5 — assessment and decisions must be based on the best available information — applies.
There is no single statutory requirement mandating a specific future projection. The national adaptation plan is principles-based rather than prescriptive, and recommends consideration of a range of plausible future conditions, and tailoring the scenario used to suit the longevity and significance of the activity being considered.
The impacts of climate change will increase the likelihood of climate-sensitive hazards such as flooding and rainfall-induced landslips. What is currently a 1% AEP event may in future become a 2% AEP event, and at the same time have increased consequences, so what is a low risk now may become a high risk in the future.
Changes to the risk profile under different climate scenarios can be assessed using the matrix in two ways: by holding the event likelihood constant and adjusting the consequence rating to reflect increased impacts; or by assessing how an event of a given magnitude may increase in likelihood over time.

[SELWYN_NH | clause NH-R2.4]
Rule — Activity status where NH-R2.3 is not met
Where compliance with NH-R2.3.a or NH-R2.3.b is not achieved, the activity is non-complying (NC).
Where compliance with NH-R2.3.c is not achieved, the activity is restricted discretionary (RDIS).
Teaching note. The two failure paths are not equivalent. Being in a high hazard area, or between a water body and its stopbank, makes the activity non-complying, which is a substantially higher consent hurdle. Failing only the certified floor level makes it restricted discretionary. A response that treats any non-compliance with NH-R2.3 as merely requiring consent understates the first case.

Natural hazard risk is also addressed through other legislation. The Building Act 2004 addresses building requirements through the Building Code and related compliance pathways. [CRPS_CH11 | clause 11.3.5]
Policy — General risk management approach
For natural hazards or areas not addressed by Policies 11.3.1, 11.3.2 and 11.3.3, subdivision, use or development of land shall be avoided if the risk from natural hazards is unacceptable.
When determining whether risk is unacceptable, the following matters will be considered: 1. the likelihood of the natural hazard event; and 2. the potential consequence of the natural hazard event for people and communities, property and infrastructure, the environment, and emergency response organisations.
Where there is uncertainty in the likelihood or consequences of a natural hazard event, the local authority shall adopt a precautionary approach.
Formal risk management techniques should be used, such as AS/NZS ISO 31000 Risk Management or AS/NZS 1170.0 Structural Design Actions.
This policy implements Objective 11.2.1.

Assessing risk for a specific activity and hazard
The NPS-NH directs a risk-based approach — assessing the risk to a specific activity from a specific natural hazard. This requires decision-makers to consider the location, type and design of the activity for which consent is sought.

Where a proposal is exposed to more than one natural hazard, the risk assessment is carried out separately for each hazard.

[NPS_NH_GUIDE_2026 | clause Disclaimer]
Status and limitations
This guide is intended to clarify policy intent and provide high-level implementation recommendations. It does not prescribe every step in natural hazard risk management.
The guide is consistent with, and expands on, the policy intent of the NPS-NH, but it is not itself part of the NPS-NH and it is not legal advice.
Read the guide alongside the National Policy Statement and other relevant official information. Where the two differ in emphasis, the National Policy Statement is the authoritative text.

[NPS_NH_GUIDE_2026 | clause Part 1: Introduction]
Purpose and organisation of the guide
The guide explains the context and scope of the NPS-NH and supports practitioners implementing it.
Part 1 explains the context and scope for the guide. Part 2 outlines the objective and policy approach of the NPS-NH. Part 3 explains what planning processes, natural hazards and activities the NPS-NH applies to. Part 4 steps through each of the implementation clauses. Part 5 explains how the NPS-NH interacts with other relevant policy instruments. Appendix 1 lists additional non-statutory reference material.
Use the guide to understand implementation intent, while using the NPS-NH itself as the authoritative national policy text.

[NPS_NH_GUIDE_2026 | clause Part 3: Consenting]
The NPS-NH is designed to apply to resource consenting
Section 104(1)(b)(iii) of the RMA requires decision-makers to have regard to any relevant national policy statement when deciding a subdivision or land-use consent. This applies until the decision is made, even if the application was lodged before the NPS-NH came into force.
For discretionary and non-complying activities, all relevant matters can be considered under section 104B, so consent authorities must have regard to relevant provisions of the NPS-NH.
Under sections 104A (controlled activities) and 104C (restricted discretionary activities), the consent authority can only consider the NPS-NH if natural hazards are explicitly included in the matters over which discretion or control has been restricted in the plan.
Sections 106 and 106A allow a consent authority to refuse consent, or grant it subject to conditions, if it considers that there is a significant risk from natural hazards. These sections can be used for any consent type.

[NPS_NH_GUIDE_2026 | clause Part 4: Climate change]
Impacts of climate change
Policy 6 requires a risk assessment to consider the potential impacts of climate change to at least 100 years into the future. The 100-year timeframe applies when using existing hazard data and information in the risk assessment process.
If information that includes climate change over the 100-year timeframe is not available or applicable, Policy 5 — assessment and decisions must be based on the best available information — applies.

The national adaptation plan is principles-based rather than prescriptive, and recommends consideration of a range of plausible future conditions, and tailoring the scenario used to suit the longevity and significance of the activity being considered.
The impacts of climate change will increase the likelihood of climate-sensitive hazards such as flooding and rainfall-induced landslips. What is currently a 1% AEP event may in future become a 2% AEP event, and at the same time have increased consequences, so what is a low risk now may become a high risk in the future.
Changes to the risk profile under different climate scenarios can be assessed using the matrix in two ways: by holding the event likelihood constant and adjusting the consequence rating to reflect increased impacts; or by assessing how an event of a given magnitude may increase in likelihood over time.

[NPS_NH_GUIDE_2026 | clause Part 4: Detailed assessment]
When a detailed risk assessment is required
Under clause 3.3(1), decision-makers must have regard to the anticipated level of risk when considering the scale and detail of information necessary.
Most natural hazard data held by a local authority is at region-wide or district-wide scale and is either susceptibility or hazard information. That type of information indicates where an area is likely to be affected by a natural hazard, and may mean that deciding an application requires a request to the applicant for a further detailed assessment of the risk by a suitably qualified and experienced person (SQEP).

Regional or district-scale susceptibility mapping is therefore an indicator of the need for further assessment, not a substitute for a site-specific assessment.

[CRPS_CH11 | clause 11.3.5]
Policy — General risk management approach
For natural hazards or areas not addressed by Policies 11.3.1, 11.3.2 and 11.3.3, subdivision, use or development of land shall be avoided if the risk from natural hazards is unacceptable.
When determining whether risk is unacceptable, the following matters will be considered: 1. the likelihood of the natural hazard event; and 2. the potential consequence of the natural hazard event for people and communities, property and infrastructure, the environment, and emergency response organisations.
Where there is uncertainty in the likelihood or consequences of a natural hazard event, the local authority shall adopt a precautionary approach.
Formal risk management techniques should be used, such as AS/NZS ISO 31000 Risk Management or AS/NZS 1170.0 Structural Design Actions.
This policy implements Objective 11.2.1.

[NPS_NH_GUIDE_2026 | clause Part 4: Climate change]

Policy 6 requires a risk assessment to consider the potential impacts of climate change to at least 100 years into the future. The 100-year timeframe applies when using existing hazard data and information in the risk assessment process.

The impacts of climate change will increase the likelihood of climate-sensitive hazards such as flooding and rainfall-induced landslips. What is currently a 1% AEP event may in future become a 2% AEP event, and at the same time have increased consequences, so what is a low risk now may become a high risk in the future.
Changes to the risk profile under different climate scenarios can be assessed using the matrix in two ways: by holding the event likelihood constant and adjusting the consequence rating to reflect increased impacts; or by assessing how an event of a given magnitude may increase in likelihood over time.

[NPS_NH_GUIDE_2026 | clause Part 3: Consenting]
The NPS-NH is designed to apply to resource consenting
Section 104(1)(b)(iii) of the RMA requires decision-makers to have regard to any relevant national policy statement when deciding a subdivision or land-use consent. This applies until the decision is made, even if the application was lodged before the NPS-NH came into force.
For discretionary and non-complying activities, all relevant matters can be considered under section 104B, so consent authorities must have regard to relevant provisions of the NPS-NH.
Under sections 104A (controlled activities) and 104C (restricted discretionary activities), the consent authority can only consider the NPS-NH if natural hazards are explicitly included in the matters over which discretion or control has been restricted in the plan.
Sections 106 and 106A allow a consent authority to refuse consent, or grant it subject to conditions, if it considers that there is a significant risk from natural hazards. These sections can be used for any consent type.

[NPS_NH_GUIDE_2026 | clause Part 5: Other instruments]
Interaction with other policies and legislation
Natural hazard risk is also addressed through other legislation. The Building Act 2004 addresses building requirements through the Building Code and related compliance pathways. The Civil Defence Emergency Management Act 2002 focuses on preparing for, responding to and recovering from natural hazard events. The Local Government Official Information and Meetings Act 1987 requires all known natural hazard information held by a council to be included on Land Information Memoranda.
Clause 1.3(3) directs that the New Zealand Coastal Policy Statement 2010 prevails where there is inconsistency in policy direction on managing natural hazard risk in the coastal environment.
Because infrastructure is exempted from the NPS-NH, the National Policy Statement for Infrastructure 2025 applies when deciding a consent for infrastructure that is not infrastructure as defined in the RMA, or where existing planning provisions manage infrastructure in areas subject to natural hazards.

[SELWYN_NH | clause NH-P11]
Policy — Defences against water
Avoid locating any residential unit or other asset of high value, including important infrastructure, between any waterbody and any defence against water designed or used to contain floodwater from that waterbody, unless that asset has a functional need or operational need to be in that location.
Teaching note. Land on the waterway side of a flood defence remains exposed to the full design event and is not protected by the defence.

[SELWYN_NH | clause NH-Overlays]
Overlays used in this chapter
The chapter manages natural hazards through mapped overlays. Those relevant to this extract are the Plains Flood Management Overlay and the Waimakariri Flood Management Overlay. The chapter also uses the Coastal Erosion Overlay, Coastal Inundation Overlay, Tsunami Policy Overlay, Greendale Fault Overlay, Fault Investigation Overlay and Fault Awareness Overlay, which are outside the scope of this assessment.
An overlay applies in addition to the underlying zone provisions. A site may therefore be subject both to the overlay provisions and to the rules of its zone.
The two flood overlays carry different rules. Within the Plains Flood Management Overlay a new residential unit or principal building is permitted subject to the NH-R2.3 conditions. Within the Waimakariri Flood Management Overlay the same activity is non-complying under NH-R2.5.
Teaching note. The presence of an overlay indicates mapped hazard exposure. The absence of an overlay on a supplied record is not evidence that a site is free from that hazard; it may mean only that the site has not been mapped, or that the supplied record does not include overlay information. The chapter itself recognises this in the Overview, which describes the Council's risk-based approach as including managing risk when there is uncertain or insufficient natural hazard risk information.

[SELWYN_NH | clause NH-REQ4.1]
Requirement — Natural hazards and earthworks
Within the Coastal Inundation Overlay, the Plains Flood Management Overlay and the Waimakariri Flood Management Overlay, the activity does not exacerbate flooding on any other property by displacing or diverting floodwater on surrounding land.
Where compliance with NH-REQ4.1 is not achieved, the activity is restricted discretionary (RDIS), with discretion restricted to NH-MAT1 Natural Hazards Generally.
Teaching note. This is the earthworks standard that gives effect to policy NH-P12. Compensatory storage or an equivalent measure is the usual way a proposal demonstrates that displaced floodwater is accommodated on site.

[SELWYN_NH | clause NH-P12]
Policy — Earthworks in flood management overlays
Manage earthworks undertaken in the Waimakariri Flood Management Overlay and the Plains Flood Management Overlay to ensure that they do not exacerbate flooding on any other property by displacing or diverting floodwater on surrounding land.
Teaching note. Earthworks that raise ground level within a flood management overlay reduce flood storage and can raise flood levels on adjoining land. The volume displaced and its effect on surrounding flood levels are the matters this policy is directed at. An assertion that a proposal will not increase flood risk on other sites is not sufficient without supporting analysis of the changed flood levels and flow paths.
