[[CLAUSE Disclaimer]]
Status and limitations
This guide is intended to clarify policy intent and provide high-level implementation recommendations. It does not prescribe every step in natural hazard risk management.
The guide is consistent with, and expands on, the policy intent of the NPS-NH, but it is not itself part of the NPS-NH and it is not legal advice.
Read the guide alongside the National Policy Statement and other relevant official information. Where the two differ in emphasis, the National Policy Statement is the authoritative text.

[[CLAUSE Part 1: Introduction]]
Purpose and organisation of the guide
The guide explains the context and scope of the NPS-NH and supports practitioners implementing it.
Part 1 explains the context and scope for the guide. Part 2 outlines the objective and policy approach of the NPS-NH. Part 3 explains what planning processes, natural hazards and activities the NPS-NH applies to. Part 4 steps through each of the implementation clauses. Part 5 explains how the NPS-NH interacts with other relevant policy instruments. Appendix 1 lists additional non-statutory reference material.
Use the guide to understand implementation intent, while using the NPS-NH itself as the authoritative national policy text.

[[CLAUSE Part 2: Aim]]
What the NPS-NH is trying to achieve
The aim of the NPS-NH is to maximise development while minimising losses from natural hazard events — in terms of injury, loss of life, and damage to land and buildings — by encouraging the right kind of development in the right locations.
To achieve this, the NPS-NH directs that a risk-based proportionate approach be applied. This means first considering the likelihood of a natural hazard event and its potential consequences for people and property, rather than just the presence of a hazard, and then applying a management response proportionate to the level of risk posed.
The NPS-NH does not direct exact outcomes, apart from the avoidance of very high risk. Management of natural hazard risk is highly context specific: communities have different risk exposure, different social and economic drivers, and different tolerance of risk.

[[CLAUSE Part 3: Consenting]]
The NPS-NH is designed to apply to resource consenting
Section 104(1)(b)(iii) of the RMA requires decision-makers to have regard to any relevant national policy statement when deciding a subdivision or land-use consent. This applies until the decision is made, even if the application was lodged before the NPS-NH came into force.
For discretionary and non-complying activities, all relevant matters can be considered under section 104B, so consent authorities must have regard to relevant provisions of the NPS-NH.
Under sections 104A (controlled activities) and 104C (restricted discretionary activities), the consent authority can only consider the NPS-NH if natural hazards are explicitly included in the matters over which discretion or control has been restricted in the plan.
Sections 106 and 106A allow a consent authority to refuse consent, or grant it subject to conditions, if it considers that there is a significant risk from natural hazards. These sections can be used for any consent type.

[[CLAUSE Part 3: Hazards]]
The NPS-NH applies to specified natural hazards
Flooding refers to inundation of land that is normally dry. It includes fluvial flooding, where waterways overflow their banks; pluvial flooding, where intense rainfall exceeds drainage capacity or soil absorption; and groundwater flooding, where the water table rises above the surface, often after prolonged rainfall.
Landslips refers to the downhill movement of soil, rock or debris under gravity, which may be triggered by rainfall or earthquake shaking. Types include rockfalls, topples, slides, flows and creep.
Coastal erosion refers to the retreat of the shoreline, temporarily or permanently, due to tides and waves including storm surges. Coastal inundation refers to inundation of land by seawater, including by storm surges and high tides.
Active faults refers to faults that have ruptured the surface in the past, as identified in the New Zealand Active Faults Database.
Liquefaction, including lateral spread, refers to loose, water-saturated soils losing strength due to earthquake shaking.
Tsunami refers to a series of long-period waves, primarily generated by underwater earthquake events where there is significant displacement of the seafloor.
Other hazards, such as volcanic activity or geothermal risks, are not specified because they require highly localised responses or are addressed through other legislation. Decision-makers may apply the NPS-NH approach to unspecified hazards but are not required to.

[[CLAUSE Part 3: Exclusions]]
Activities excluded — infrastructure and primary production
The NPS-NH applies to all activities managed under the RMA except infrastructure, as defined in section 2(1) of the RMA, and primary production, as defined in the National Planning Standards. These activities are excluded because they require a more nuanced management approach.
Infrastructure under section 2(1) of the RMA includes: pipelines distributing or transmitting gas, petroleum, biofuel or geothermal energy; telecommunication and radiocommunication networks; electricity generation facilities and lines; water supply distribution systems including irrigation; drainage or sewerage systems; structures for transport on land by cycleway, rail, road or walkway; facilities for loading or unloading cargo or passengers; aerodromes and navigation installations; port facilities; and anything described as a network utility operation in regulations.
Primary production under the National Planning Standards means any aquaculture, agricultural, pastoral, horticultural, mining, quarrying or forestry activity; includes initial processing, as an ancillary activity, of commodities resulting from those activities, and the land and buildings used for that production and initial processing; but excludes further processing of those commodities into a different product.
Any infrastructure not covered by the RMA definition is subject to the NPS-NH. The definition of infrastructure in the National Policy Statement for Infrastructure 2025 is for that instrument alone and should not be adopted when applying the NPS-NH.
The exclusion is not intended to be limiting. Local authorities can still apply an existing approach for managing natural hazard risk to infrastructure and primary production activities.

[[CLAUSE Part 3: Existing provisions]]
The NPS-NH does not automatically override existing planning provisions
The NPS-NH sets a national baseline for managing natural hazard risk through land-use planning. Many district plans already include risk-based approaches that use different likelihood thresholds, consequence measures and risk matrices.
The NPS-NH does not seek to replace these approaches where they are more conservative — see clause 1.3(4). Where an existing planning approach would prevent development that the NPS-NH risk matrix would assess as lower than very high risk, decision-makers may choose to apply those planning provisions rather than follow the NPS-NH.
Where a plan includes existing risk-based provisions for natural hazards, a first step should be assessing them against the NPS-NH approach to determine whether they are more permissive, the same, or more conservative.

[[CLAUSE Part 4: Assess the risk]]
Assessing risk for a specific activity and hazard
The NPS-NH directs a risk-based approach — assessing the risk to a specific activity from a specific natural hazard. This requires decision-makers to consider the location, type and design of the activity for which consent is sought.
Clause 3.2 of the NPS-NH identifies the key components required in a risk-based assessment: the likelihood of the event, the consequence for life and property, and consideration of both mitigation measures and residual risk.
Where a proposal is exposed to more than one natural hazard, the risk assessment is carried out separately for each hazard.

[[CLAUSE Part 4: Likelihood]]
Determining the likelihood level
Likelihood is the chance of something happening. The likelihood table uses two measures: probability of exceedance — the probability that a hazard event of a certain size will occur or be exceeded in a given time period, referred to as annual exceedance probability (AEP) when that period is one year; and average recurrence interval (ARI), the average period between hazard events of a given magnitude, often called a return period.
The likelihood table includes seven levels based on the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change likelihood scale, adjusted for New Zealand natural hazard management terminology. Qualitative descriptors range from almost certain to very rare.
District plans often do not seek to manage events with a likelihood more frequent than 1% AEP, and councils may not hold data on those events. In these circumstances the use of best available information may be relied on.
If a council holds information for a particular natural hazard at a range of likelihoods, it should manage the impacts of the likelihood event that results in the highest level of risk under the matrix.

[[CLAUSE Part 4: Consequence]]
Determining the consequence level
Consequence generally refers to the impact of a natural hazard event on the built, social, economic or natural environments. The NPS-NH consequence table focuses on consequences for property — buildings and land — and for people, in terms of injuries or fatalities.
The severity of the consequences from a natural hazard is a function of the characteristics of the hazard, such as magnitude and duration; the exposure of the elements at risk, that is, what is in the way; and the vulnerability of the elements at risk, such as building design or the age of an individual.
When assessing consequence, if a different consequence level results for each column of the table, the level of risk should be determined by applying the highest consequence level in the risk matrix.

[[CLAUSE Part 4: Risk level]]
Combining likelihood and consequence
Policy 1 directs that the risk level must be assessed using the risk matrix. The matrix combines the likelihood and consequence of a particular natural hazard event to determine whether the level of natural hazard risk is low, medium, high or very high.
The matrix is a deterministic lookup. It should not be re-derived, approximated or reasoned about qualitatively when the likelihood and consequence levels are known.

[[CLAUSE Part 4: Mitigation]]
Mitigation measures
Clause 3.2(2)(a) directs that an assessment of natural hazard risk must consider the impact of existing and proposed mitigation measures.
This ensures the risk assessment fully considers the proposal, and encourages proponents of development to include site-level mitigation measures where those will reduce the risk from natural hazards.

[[CLAUSE Part 4: Residual risk]]
Residual risk
Clause 3.2(2)(b) requires decision-makers to consider the residual risk associated with any existing or proposed mitigation measures. Residual risk is the risk that remains after risk treatment measures — avoidance or mitigation — have been applied.
Any new development in hazard-prone areas, even with mitigation measures, will result in an increase in exposure and an increase in overall risk.
At the consenting scale, it is most often hard structural measures, such as catch nets for slope instability, or design measures, such as minimum floor levels for flooding, that are proposed. These are designed to protect against a particular likelihood event, and residual risk arises from the potential occurrence of an event beyond the design threshold of the mitigation measure.
Decision-makers should be aware that extreme events exceeding design thresholds are possible, and may need to prepare emergency management plans and response strategies. Intensifying housing in areas protected by hard structures may increase risk to life in an extreme event that overtops defences.

[[CLAUSE Part 4: Climate change]]
Impacts of climate change
Policy 6 requires a risk assessment to consider the potential impacts of climate change to at least 100 years into the future. The 100-year timeframe applies when using existing hazard data and information in the risk assessment process.
If information that includes climate change over the 100-year timeframe is not available or applicable, Policy 5 — assessment and decisions must be based on the best available information — applies.
There is no single statutory requirement mandating a specific future projection. The national adaptation plan is principles-based rather than prescriptive, and recommends consideration of a range of plausible future conditions, and tailoring the scenario used to suit the longevity and significance of the activity being considered.
The impacts of climate change will increase the likelihood of climate-sensitive hazards such as flooding and rainfall-induced landslips. What is currently a 1% AEP event may in future become a 2% AEP event, and at the same time have increased consequences, so what is a low risk now may become a high risk in the future.
Changes to the risk profile under different climate scenarios can be assessed using the matrix in two ways: by holding the event likelihood constant and adjusting the consequence rating to reflect increased impacts; or by assessing how an event of a given magnitude may increase in likelihood over time.

[[CLAUSE Part 4: Proportionate management]]
Managing risk proportionately
The NPS-NH directs decision-makers to respond proportionately to natural hazard risk. When risk is higher, stronger constraints on development are appropriate; when risk is low, development should be enabled.
The intended outcome is that new development likely to be badly damaged by natural hazards does not progress, and that any limitation on, or required mitigation measure for, new development is justified.
Clause 3.3(2) provides the framework: very high risk is avoided; high or medium risk is avoided or mitigated proportionate to the level of risk; and any creation of, or increase in, significant risk on other sites is avoided or mitigated proportionate to the level of risk.
The NPS-NH is not intended to limit the way decision-makers manage risks below the very high threshold. Policies and rules in existing plans that avoid new development at a risk level below very high continue to apply.

[[CLAUSE Part 4: Other sites]]
Managing risk on other sites
Clause 3.3(2)(c) requires that subdivision, use or development, including any associated mitigation measures, avoids or mitigates the creation of, or increase in, significant risk on other sites.
Whether that risk is to be avoided or mitigated depends on what response is proportionate to the level of risk posed.
Mitigation works that redirect or displace floodwater are a common way in which a proposal can create or increase risk on a neighbouring site.

[[CLAUSE Part 4: Significant risk]]
What counts as significant natural hazard risk
Significant natural hazard risk is defined for the purposes of the NPS-NH as natural hazard risk assessed as medium, high or very high using the risk matrix.
This definition is based on a common understanding of significance — something large or important enough to warrant attention. A significant risk from natural hazards is therefore one requiring some form of management response, including avoidance or mitigation.
A risk assessed as low is not significant natural hazard risk for the purposes of the NPS-NH.

[[CLAUSE Part 4: Cost effectiveness]]
Cost effectiveness of mitigation measures
Clause 3.3(3) requires decision-makers, when determining whether mitigation measures are a proportionate response to the risk posed, to consider their cost effectiveness.
This may include consideration of the technical feasibility of the mitigation measures, the longevity of the measures and the level of service provided over time, and any ongoing maintenance or monitoring requirements.

[[CLAUSE Part 4: Detailed assessment]]
When a detailed risk assessment is required
Under clause 3.3(1), decision-makers must have regard to the anticipated level of risk when considering the scale and detail of information necessary.
Most natural hazard data held by a local authority is at region-wide or district-wide scale and is either susceptibility or hazard information. That type of information indicates where an area is likely to be affected by a natural hazard, and may mean that deciding an application requires a request to the applicant for a further detailed assessment of the risk by a suitably qualified and experienced person (SQEP).
Any risk assessment prepared by an SQEP to support a consent application should align with the NPS-NH framework. Depending on the level of risk, it may also be appropriate for the consent authority to have the applicant's risk assessment peer reviewed by an SQEP before deciding the application.
As a general guide, an SQEP is a person who is independent; has a relevant professional registration, such as chartered professional engineer; applies good professional practice; undertakes a risk assessment in accordance with accepted industry practice and guidelines; and is willing to certify and stand by the conclusions of the report.
Regional or district-scale susceptibility mapping is therefore an indicator of the need for further assessment, not a substitute for a site-specific assessment.

[[CLAUSE Part 4: Best available information]]
Applying the best available information
Clause 3.4 seeks to ensure decision-making is based on the best information available at the time. This means using complete and scientifically robust data where possible, but it does not preclude using information that is uncertain or incomplete.
The direction to use the best available information allows decision-makers to actively manage risks even when the information is not perfect. The alternative — delaying action because of uncertainty about the exact risk profile — means people and property stay at risk.
Applying best available information involves: using complete and scientifically robust data where possible; recognising that best available information may include modelling output, partial data and information from other sources; giving preference, when information is uncertain or incomplete, to sources providing the greatest level of certainty while taking all practicable steps to reduce uncertainty; and interpreting uncertain information in a way that gives effect to the proportionate risk-based approach.
Local authorities should develop their own criteria for evaluating the suitability of information — for example, the age of the information; advances in science or changes in the environment since publication; the qualifications and experience of the author; whether it was peer reviewed; whether it follows best practice methods; and whether uncertainties and limitations are well documented.
Where two sources of hazard information for the same site disagree, these criteria — currency, method, review status and documented uncertainty — are the basis for deciding which is the better available information. The disagreement itself should be recorded rather than resolved silently.

[[CLAUSE Part 5: Other instruments]]
Interaction with other policies and legislation
Natural hazard risk is also addressed through other legislation. The Building Act 2004 addresses building requirements through the Building Code and related compliance pathways. The Civil Defence Emergency Management Act 2002 focuses on preparing for, responding to and recovering from natural hazard events. The Local Government Official Information and Meetings Act 1987 requires all known natural hazard information held by a council to be included on Land Information Memoranda.
Clause 1.3(3) directs that the New Zealand Coastal Policy Statement 2010 prevails where there is inconsistency in policy direction on managing natural hazard risk in the coastal environment.
Because infrastructure is exempted from the NPS-NH, the National Policy Statement for Infrastructure 2025 applies when deciding a consent for infrastructure that is not infrastructure as defined in the RMA, or where existing planning provisions manage infrastructure in areas subject to natural hazards.
The NPS-NH does not have precedence over the National Policy Statement on Urban Development 2020. Both must be considered and the outcomes sought by each balanced.
